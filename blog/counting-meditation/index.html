Counting meditation: the technique you cannot get wrong.

It is the oldest beginner's instruction in Zen and one of the few meditation methods researchers can measure. It also asks almost nothing of you.

Most meditation advice arrives with a lot of atmosphere. Observe without judgement. Rest in awareness. Let thoughts pass like clouds. All of it is true and none of it tells you what to do with the next thirty seconds. Counting does. You breathe out, you think "one". You breathe out, you think "two". You get to ten, or you lose your place somewhere around four, and either way you go back to one.

That is the whole method. It has been taught to beginners for well over a thousand years, it is the one meditation technique that researchers have been able to turn into a measurable task, and it is almost impossible to do wrong, because doing it wrong is built into the instructions.

Why counting works

Attention needs something to hold. The usual instruction, follow the breath, gives it something subtle, and subtle is hard when you are new, tired or anxious. A number is not subtle. It is a small, definite job for the thinking mind, which is the part that otherwise wanders off to plan dinner. The count keeps that part busy enough to stay, without being interesting enough to take over.

Counting also gives you a built-in signal. With plain breath-watching, you can drift for five minutes and not know it. With counting, the moment you notice you are on fourteen, or that you have no idea what number you were on, you have caught the mind wandering. That noticing is the skill meditation is meant to train, and counting makes it visible.

The number is not the point. Noticing when the number has gone is the point.

Three ways to count

There are many variants. These three cover most of what people need, and they get progressively more demanding in a useful way.

1

One to ten on the out-breath

The classic. Breathe in naturally, and on each out-breath count silently: one, two, up to ten. At ten, start again at one. If you lose your place, or find yourself past ten, go back to one without comment.

2

Backwards from twenty

Same rhythm, but start at twenty and count down on each out-breath. Counting down is slightly harder for the brain, so it is a better hook when the mind is busy, and arriving at one feels like a small landing.

3

Draw the number

Instead of thinking the word, picture each number being slowly drawn in your mind's eye, one stroke at a time, as if by a brush. It can sit on the breath or go at its own pace. Good for visual people, and for anyone who finds watching the breath uncomfortable.

The third variant deserves a note. Not everyone can use the breath as an anchor; for some people with anxiety, asthma or a trauma history, close attention to breathing makes things worse rather than better. Drawing numbers in the mind's eye gives attention a steady object that is nowhere near the chest. If that sounds like you, we have written more about it in when focusing on the breath makes it worse.

Losing count is the practice

Here is the part that gets most people. You will lose count, often, and you will probably feel a small sting each time, as if you had failed a test with only ten questions. The sting is worth examining, because it is the same reflex that makes meditation feel like one more thing to be bad at.

Losing count is not a failure of the method. It is the method working. Every time you return to one, you have done a complete repetition of the thing you are practising: attention left, you noticed, attention came back. Someone who loses count thirty times in ten minutes has practised thirty times. Someone who sails to ten on autopilot while thinking about email has practised nothing, and may not even know it. The Zen teachers who taught this were not running a counting competition. They were giving students a way to see their own minds, and the lost count is exactly where the mind becomes visible.

Thirty lost counts in ten minutes is thirty repetitions, not thirty failures.

Where it comes from

In Zen the practice is called susokukan, literally observing by counting the breath, and it is traditionally the first thing given to a new student before they move on to following the breath without numbers and eventually to sitting with nothing at all. Its roots are older than Zen: breath-counting sits among the earliest Buddhist meditation instructions, and in the yogic tradition pranayama uses counts to set the length of each inhale, hold and exhale. Different traditions, the same discovery. A number is the simplest thing you can give a mind that will not stay still.

What the research says

Meditation research has a measurement problem: most of it relies on people reporting how mindful they feel, which is not the same as being mindful. Counting offered a way around it. In 2014, Daniel Levinson and colleagues at the University of Wisconsin, working in Richard Davidson's lab, published a set of four studies with over 400 participants in Frontiers in Psychology. People counted their breaths in cycles of nine, pressing one key for breaths one to eight and a different key for the ninth, so the computer could tell when they lost count.

Accuracy on the task lined up with self-reported mindfulness, separated long-term meditators from matched non-meditators, and was linked to less mind-wandering and better mood. In a randomised online study, four weeks of breath-counting practice improved mindfulness scores and reduced mind-wandering compared with both a working-memory task and no training. A fair reading: these are modest effects in healthy adults, and a 2025 paper has since questioned how well the task works for people who ruminate heavily. But the core result stands. Counting is not a toy version of meditation. It is a measurable way of training the same skill.

Your first sit

Sit where you will not be interrupted, eyes closed or lowered. Set a timer for five minutes, and turn the interval bells off so there is nothing to wait for. Luvia's meditation timer in rituals does this in two taps, and keeps your sits together with your other small rituals. Let the breath be whatever it is, then begin: out-breath, one. Out-breath, two. When you lose your place, back to one, no commentary.

Stay with the first variant for a week before trying the others. When one to ten begins to feel easy, that is the moment to count backwards from twenty, and when that is easy, to drop the numbers and just follow the breath. Some people never drop them, and that is fine too. The count is not a stepping stone you are supposed to leave behind. It is a place you can always return to, on the days when the mind is loud and you need a small, definite thing to hold.

The short answers

How does counting meditation work?

You breathe naturally and count each out-breath silently from one to ten, then start again at one. When you lose your place or notice you have gone past ten, you return to one without judgement. The count gives the thinking mind a small job and makes it obvious when attention has wandered.

Is it better to count forwards or backwards?

Both work. Counting one to ten on the out-breath is the classic and the best place to start. Counting backwards from twenty is slightly more demanding, which makes it a stronger hook on days when the mind is busy. Try forwards for a week before switching.

What do I do when I lose count?

Go back to one. Losing count is not a failure; it is the moment you noticed the mind had wandered, which is exactly the skill the practice trains. Each return is one complete repetition.

Is there any science behind counting breaths?

Yes. A 2014 set of studies from the University of Wisconsin validated breath counting as a behavioural measure of mindfulness, and found that four weeks of counting practice improved mindfulness and reduced mind-wandering compared with control groups. The effects were modest and measured in healthy adults.
