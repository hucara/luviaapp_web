How to meditate for the first time: a five-minute version.

No lineage, no app full of courses, no right posture. Five minutes, one anchor, and a realistic idea of what is supposed to happen.

5 minute read

Most people who want to try meditation never get as far as sitting down. Not because it is hard, but because the instructions they find come wrapped in something else: a tradition to choose, a posture to get right, a voice to follow, a ten-day course to commit to. All of that can come later, or never. The first session needs almost none of it.

What follows is the simplest version that still counts. It takes five minutes, it works on a kitchen chair, and it does not ask you to believe anything. If you do it once and decide it is not for you, you will have lost five minutes. If you do it for a couple of weeks, there is decent evidence that something shifts.

The five steps

1

Sit

Any chair, any floor. Upright enough to stay awake, relaxed enough to stay put. Hands wherever they fall.

2

Set a timer

Five minutes, with a soft bell at the end. The timer does the clock-watching so you do not have to.

3

Pick one anchor

Usually the breath, felt at the nose or the belly. Rest your attention there, lightly. Do not control it.

4

Expect to wander

Within seconds you will be somewhere else: lunch, an email, the timer. This is not a failure. It is the exercise.

5

Come back

When you notice you have drifted, return to the anchor without comment. Repeat until the bell.

Sit, set a bell, choose one anchor, wander, return. Everything else is optional.

Wandering is the rep

The single most useful thing to know before your first session is that the mind will leave, and that noticing it has left is the whole point. Beginners tend to treat each drift as proof they are doing it wrong, and by the third or fourth one they have quietly concluded that they are not a meditation person. The opposite is closer to the truth. Every time you catch yourself mid-thought and return to the breath, you have done one repetition of the thing meditation trains. A session with thirty returns was not thirty failures. It was thirty reps.

You do not meditate by having no thoughts. You meditate by noticing you had one, and coming back.

It helps to make the return deliberately plain. No sigh, no verdict, no resolution to concentrate harder. Just the noticing, and then the breath again. The tone you use on yourself in that moment turns out to matter more than your posture or the length of the session, because it is the tone you will carry into every other session.

What a normal first session feels like

Usually a little boring, a little restless, and longer than five minutes has any right to feel. Some people notice their heart rate or a small itch and find it oddly loud. Some get a wave of sleepiness at the two-minute mark. A few feel calmer, and a few feel more agitated because they have finally stopped moving long enough to notice how busy their head is. All of these are ordinary. None of them tells you much about whether meditation will suit you, any more than a first run tells you whether you could enjoy running.

What you are unlikely to feel is the stillness people describe on retreat. That comes, if it comes, from practice rather than from a good first attempt, and chasing it in week one is a reliable way to quit. The realistic goal for a first session is simply to reach the bell. The realistic goal for the first fortnight is to reach the bell most days.

How often, and for how long

Short and daily beats long and occasional, and this is one of the better-supported claims in the field. In a randomised trial at New York University, people who had never meditated did thirteen minutes of guided practice a day for eight weeks, against a control group who listened to a podcast for the same time. The meditators ended up with better attention and working memory, lower negative mood, and lower anxiety under a standard lab stress test. Notably, the same group measured after four weeks showed little change; the effects appeared with the second month.

A larger and more recent trial from the University of Bath and the University of Southampton randomised 1,247 adults to either ten minutes a day of app-based mindfulness for thirty days or to a control condition. The mindfulness group reported lower depression and anxiety scores and better wellbeing and sleep, and most of that was still present a month after the programme ended. Ten minutes is short enough that the question becomes whether you will actually do it, rather than whether it is enough.

So start at five, because five is easy to defend on a bad day, and let it grow to ten when you stop checking how long is left. Morning tends to survive better than evening, because the evening is where good intentions go to be tired. Attaching it to something you already do, the kettle or the shower or closing the laptop, helps more than any amount of motivation.

If the breath is not a good anchor

For a meaningful number of people, attending to the breath makes things worse rather than better. Watching it can make it feel effortful, and for anyone with a history of panic or certain kinds of anxiety the breath is the last thing they want to stare at. This is common, it is not a sign that meditation is off the table, and it has simple fixes. The anchor can be the feeling of your hands, the pressure of the chair, or sounds in the room. Counting breaths in a cycle of ten gives the mind a little more to hold, which some people find steadier than breath alone; there is a short guide to counting meditation if you want that version. And if breath focus actively unsettles you, there is a separate piece on what to do when breath focus makes it worse.

The short answers

How long should a first meditation be?

Five minutes is enough. The aim of the first session is to reach the closing bell, not to feel anything in particular. Most people can grow to ten minutes within a couple of weeks, which is the length used in several randomised trials.

What do I do when my mind wanders?

Notice it, and return your attention to the anchor without judging yourself. That noticing and returning is the exercise itself, so a session with many returns is a good session rather than a failed one.

How often should a beginner meditate?

Daily if you can, even briefly. Trials of ten to thirteen minutes a day over four to eight weeks found improvements in mood, attention and anxiety, and shorter daily practice tends to survive longer than longer occasional practice.

What if focusing on my breath makes me anxious?

That is common and does not rule meditation out. Use a different anchor, such as the feeling of your hands, sounds in the room, or counting breaths in cycles of ten. If breath focus reliably makes things worse, try a body or sound anchor instead.
