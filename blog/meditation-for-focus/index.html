Meditation for focus: what ten minutes a day does to attention.

Focused-attention meditation is attention training in the most literal sense: notice you have wandered, come back, repeat. Here is what that does over weeks, and how to do it.

Most people who search for meditation for focus are not looking for calm. They want to read a page without reaching the bottom and realising they absorbed none of it, or to work on one thing for forty minutes without the pull toward the next tab. The honest answer is that meditation can help with this, the evidence is real, and it is also smaller and slower than the way it is usually sold. This post lays out both halves and then gives you a plain ten-minute practice to try.

How often the mind is elsewhere

In 2010, Matthew Killingsworth and Daniel Gilbert used a phone app to ping more than two thousand people at random moments through the day and ask what they were doing and what they were thinking about. In roughly 47 per cent of samples, people were thinking about something other than what they were doing. Mind-wandering was the norm across almost every activity, and it predicted lower mood in the moment, regardless of whether the wandering was pleasant. The study is not about meditation, but it sets the baseline: attention drifting away is not a flaw you have, it is the default setting, and focus is the exception that has to be practised.

Attention wanders about half the time. Training focus is not about stopping that. It is about noticing sooner and returning more easily.

The rep you are training

Focused-attention meditation has one instruction. Rest your attention on something simple, usually the breath. When you notice it has gone elsewhere, which it will within seconds, bring it back without comment. That is the whole practice, and the part that matters is not the resting. It is the noticing and the returning. Each time you catch the drift and come back, you have done one repetition of the exact skill you wanted: detecting that you are off task and re-engaging. Ten minutes might contain thirty or forty such reps, so a mind that wanders constantly is not failing. It is getting more practice.

You are not learning to breathe. You are building the habit of checking where your attention is and moving it on purpose.

What the studies show

Several controlled studies, all with people who had never meditated before, point in the same direction.

Fadel Zeidan and colleagues in 2010 gave people four sessions of twenty minutes of focused-breathing training, with a control group that listened to an audiobook instead. Both groups felt better afterwards, but only the meditation group improved on tasks of working memory and sustained attention.

Michael Mrazek and colleagues in 2013 ran a two-week mindfulness course against a nutrition course at the University of California, Santa Barbara. The meditation group improved on a working-memory measure and on GRE reading comprehension, and reported less mind-wandering during the tests. The gain was largest in those who had been most distractible at the start.

Julia Basso, Wendy Suzuki and colleagues in 2019 randomised novices to thirteen minutes a day of guided meditation or thirteen minutes of a podcast, for eight weeks. At four weeks the groups did not differ. At eight weeks the meditation group showed better attention, working memory and recognition memory, along with lower anxiety and fatigue. The detail worth remembering is the timeline: a month of daily practice was not enough to separate the groups, two months was.

Amishi Jha, Jason Krompinger and Michael Baime in 2007 tested an eight-week mindfulness course using the Attention Network Test, which breaks attention into alerting, orienting and conflict monitoring. The course group improved specifically in orienting, the ability to direct attention where you choose, compared with controls, while a group on an intensive retreat improved in alerting instead. Different doses seemed to train different parts of the system.

How big the effect is

Three honest caveats. The effects in these studies are modest. They are statistically reliable differences on laboratory measures and one exam-style test, not a transformation of how you work. They arrive after weeks of regular practice, not days, and the one study that checked the halfway point found nothing there yet. And they depend on actually doing it: the people who improved were practising most days, and there is no reason to expect a few sessions scattered across a month to show the same. Meditation for concentration is closer to going for a run than to taking a tablet. The gains are earned slowly, they fade if you stop, and they are worth having anyway.

Real, modest, slow, and dependent on showing up. That is the whole claim.

A ten-minute protocol

Sit somewhere you can stay upright without effort. Set a timer for ten minutes and put it out of sight; a silent one is best, so you are not waiting for it. Luvia's meditation timer runs silent by default, with start and end bells if you want them. Close your eyes or lower your gaze. Find the breath wherever it is clearest, at the nostrils or the belly, and rest your attention there. Do not control it. When you notice you have drifted, and you will, name it lightly if that helps, something like thinking, and return to the breath. Repeat until the timer ends. That is it. There is no state to reach and no feeling that means it worked.

If the bare breath gives your mind too little to hold, count. One on the out-breath, up to ten, then start again, and back to one whenever you lose the number. Counting is a scaffold that makes the moment of drifting obvious, which is exactly what you want to practise noticing. We wrote a full guide to counting meditation if you want the details, including what to do when you keep arriving at fourteen.

Ten minutes a day, most days, for at least eight weeks before you judge it. Mark the sessions somewhere: consistency is the ingredient, and a visible streak is the cheapest way to protect it.

The phone, before you sit

The most common way a focus practice fails is not inside the ten minutes. It is in the thirty seconds before, when you open the timer app and a notification arrives and the session never starts. So decide the phone's place before you sit. If the timer is on it, switch on do not disturb, turn it face down, and put it an arm's length away rather than in your lap. If you are using a separate timer, leave the phone in another room. The point is removing the cue, so the only decision left is to start.

Pairing it with a WOOP

Meditation trains the general skill of noticing and returning. It does not, on its own, tell you what to do about the specific thing that pulls you off task at eleven in the morning. For that, a short WOOP on distraction is a good companion. Name the wish (finish the report before lunch), the outcome (the afternoon free of it), the obstacle you actually face (reaching for the phone when a paragraph gets hard) and an if-then plan for that exact moment. The meditation makes you better at catching the reach; the plan tells you what to do once you have. There is a dedicated post on WOOP for focus, and both the timer and WOOP live together in Luvia's rituals, which makes it easy to do one after the other.

Ten quiet minutes will not make you a different person by Friday. Over a couple of months, it may make you someone who notices the drift sooner and comes back more easily. In a mind that wanders half the time, that is not a small thing.

The short answers

Does meditation improve attention?

In controlled studies with beginners, regular focused-attention meditation improved measures of sustained attention, working memory and mind-wandering compared with control activities. The effects are real but modest, and they appeared after weeks of near-daily practice rather than days.

How long does it take for meditation to improve focus?

Some lab measures moved after four sessions, but the most careful study found no difference at four weeks and a clear one at eight weeks of thirteen minutes a day. Plan on at least two months of regular practice before judging it.

How many minutes of meditation a day for concentration?

Ten to fifteen minutes a day is the range most studies used. Consistency matters more than length; ten minutes most days beats an hour once a week.

Is my meditation working if my mind keeps wandering?

Yes. Noticing the wander and returning is the repetition that trains attention. A session full of drifting and returning is a session full of practice, not a failed one.
