When focusing on the breath makes it worse.

Most meditation instructions begin with the breath. For some people that is the one place attention should not start, and there are good alternatives.

You sit down, close your eyes, and do what every app and every teacher says: bring your attention to the breath. Within a minute the breath feels wrong. Too shallow, then too deliberate, then impossible to do without doing it on purpose. Your chest tightens. Your heart picks up. By the time the bell rings you are more wound up than when you started, and the quiet conclusion is that meditation is for other people.

It is worth saying plainly that this is common, that it has been described in the research literature for over forty years, and that it does not mean you cannot meditate. It means the breath is the wrong anchor for you, at least for now. Attention needs somewhere to rest, and the breath is only one of the places it can go.

The breath is not neutral for everyone

The breath is the default anchor because it is always available, it is free, and it sits halfway between the body and the mind. For most people it is also fairly boring, which is precisely what makes it a good object to return to. But for a meaningful minority, the breath is anything but boring. If you have lived with panic, every change in your breathing is a possible alarm, and watching it closely is like staring at a smoke detector. If you have asthma or another breathing condition, the breath is a thing you monitor for safety, not a thing you can observe idly. If you carry trauma, turning attention inward and sitting still can bring the body's stored alarm to the surface rather than settling it.

In all of these cases the instruction to follow the breath asks you to pay close, sustained attention to the exact signal your nervous system is already on guard about. Attention amplifies what it lands on. So the breath becomes more noticeable, more effortful and more uncomfortable, and the discomfort reads as failure. David Treleaven, whose book Trauma-Sensitive Mindfulness is the standard reference for teachers on this, recommends that people in this position choose a different anchor altogether rather than push through, and that eyes-open practice and shorter sits are sensible defaults.

If the breath makes you more anxious, that is information about the anchor, not a verdict on you.

What the research says

The effect has a name, relaxation-induced anxiety, and it was documented in 1983 by Heide and Borkovec, who found that a portion of tense participants became measurably more anxious during relaxation training and mantra meditation, not less. It has since been studied as part of a broader picture. A 2020 systematic review by Farias and colleagues in Acta Psychiatrica Scandinavica looked at 83 studies of meditation practices and found that anxiety was the most commonly reported adverse event, followed by low mood and changes in thinking. A 2021 study by Willoughby Britton's group, using a structured interview after an eight-week mindfulness course, found that most participants reported at least one meditation-related side effect, the majority of them mild and short-lived, and a smaller group reported lasting difficulty.

Two honest caveats. These studies mostly cover full mindfulness programmes and intensive retreats, not five minutes on a timer. And unpleasant is not the same as harmful: a wave of discomfort that passes is part of practice for many people. What the evidence does establish is that meditation is not automatically calming, that some people are more likely to find it activating, and that the sensible response is to adjust the practice rather than to blame yourself or abandon it.

Six other anchors

An anchor is simply the thing you return to when you notice you have drifted. Here are six that work for people who find the breath uncomfortable, roughly in the order I would suggest trying them.

1

Contact points

The soles of your feet on the floor, the weight of your body in the chair, your hands resting on your thighs. Pressure and temperature are steady, outside the chest, and hard to get wrong.

2

Sound

Let hearing be the anchor. Traffic, a fridge, birds, the room's own hum. Sounds arrive and leave on their own, which makes them a gentle lesson in not controlling anything.

3

A silent count

Count slowly from one to ten in your head, then start again. The count can ride on the breath or stand alone, at whatever pace feels unhurried.

4

A phrase

A short phrase or word repeated quietly, the way a mantra works. Something neutral or kind: "here", "this moment", "let it be". The words hold attention so the breath does not have to.

5

Eyes open, soft gaze

Rest your eyes on a point a metre or two ahead, slightly lowered, without staring. Keeping the room in view tells the nervous system you are not trapped.

6

Walking

Slow walking with attention on the feet, or an ordinary walk with attention on what you see. Movement gives the body something to do, which for many anxious people is the difference.

A seventh option is a short body scan that deliberately skips the chest: attention moves from the feet up to the knees, the hands, the shoulders and the face, and back down. It keeps the inward turn that makes meditation useful while staying away from the one region that sets off the alarm.

How to choose one

Pick by what your nervous system already treats as safe. If being still is the hard part, choose walking. If the inward turn is the hard part, choose sound or an open gaze, both of which point attention outward. If your mind needs something to do or it panics, choose the count or the phrase, which give it a small job. If you simply want the breath's steadiness without the breath, choose contact points. There is no ranking here, and no anchor is more advanced than another. Counting, in particular, has a long and respectable history, and we have written about it separately in counting meditation: the technique you cannot get wrong.

Then hold the choice lightly. Try one anchor for a week of short sits rather than a new one each day, because part of what you are learning is what it feels like to return to this particular place. If it is clearly wrong after a few days, that is a finding, and you move on.

Attention needs somewhere to rest. The breath is one option, not the rule.

Trying it on a silent timer

A silent timer is better: you choose the anchor, the timer holds the time, and nothing interrupts. Luvia's meditation timer lives in rituals and lets you set the length and turn the interval bells off, so there is nothing to brace for. Start with three or four minutes, not twenty. Sit or stand where you can see the door. Keep your eyes open if that feels safer. Settle on your anchor, notice when you have drifted, and come back without commentary.

If anxiety rises anyway, you do not have to sit through it. Open your eyes, look around the room, name three things you can see, feel your feet, and stop the timer if you need to. Stopping early is part of the practice when it is chosen, not a failure of it. Over time most people find the window widens on its own.

The kinder reading

The story people tell themselves after a bad sit is that they are too anxious to meditate, as if meditation were a reward for people who are already calm. The research points the other way. Meditation is a set of ways to place attention, and the breath is only the most common of them. If it makes things worse, the response is to choose a different place, not to conclude that you are the exception. Your feet, the sound of the room, a quiet count, a kind word repeated: any of these can be the thing you come back to. That coming back is the whole practice, wherever you do it from.

The short answers

Why does focusing on my breath make me more anxious?

Attention amplifies whatever it lands on. If you have a history of panic, a breathing condition, or trauma, your nervous system already treats the breath as a signal to watch, so close attention to it tends to increase alarm rather than settle it. Researchers have described this as relaxation-induced anxiety since 1983.

Can you meditate without focusing on the breath?

Yes. The breath is one anchor among many. Contact points such as the feet and hands, ambient sound, a silent count, a repeated phrase, an eyes-open soft gaze and slow walking all work as places to return attention to, and teachers who work with trauma often recommend them as the default.

Is it dangerous to meditate if I have anxiety?

For most people it is not, but meditation is not automatically calming, and studies show a minority of people find it activating. Start with short sits, keep your eyes open if that feels safer, choose an anchor other than the breath, and stop if distress rises. If you have a diagnosed condition or trauma history, it is reasonable to talk it through with a clinician first.

Should I use a guided meditation or a timer?

If the breath is the problem, a silent timer with bells off gives you more control, because most guided recordings return to the breath repeatedly. Choose your own anchor, set three to five minutes, and let the timer hold the time.
