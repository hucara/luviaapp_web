Why being kind to a stranger helps you too.

The studies are clear that kindness lifts the giver. They are also clear about when it does not, and that part is worth reading closely.

Most of us have had the experience of doing something small for someone else and feeling, briefly and slightly absurdly, better than the something warranted. Holding a door, sending the message you kept meaning to send, telling a colleague they handled a bad meeting well. The feeling is real, and it has been measured often enough that we can say roughly how big it is, who it works for, and, more usefully, when it quietly fails.

What kindness does for the giver

In 2018 Oliver Curry and colleagues pulled together every experiment they could find in which people were asked to perform acts of kindness and then had their wellbeing measured against a control group: 27 studies, just over four thousand participants. The combined effect was small to medium, a standardised difference of about 0.28. That is an honest number. It is not a cure for anything, and it is roughly the same size as many things people already do on purpose to feel better. What makes it notable is the cost, which is close to zero, and the consistency, which held across ages, countries and kinds of kindness.

Others, not yourself

A reasonable objection is that anything pleasant would do the same job. Katherine Nelson and colleagues tested exactly that in 2016 with 473 adults over six weeks. One group did acts of kindness for other people, one did acts of kindness for the world, one did kind things for themselves, and one simply tracked their days. The two outward-facing groups ended the study with higher psychological flourishing, carried by more positive and less negative emotion. The group that treated themselves well ended up where the control group did. Self-care has its place, but the lift in these studies comes specifically from turning towards someone else.

The lift does not come from doing something nice. It comes from doing it for someone.

The loneliness problem

Here is where the research gets more careful, and where an app built around anonymous kindness has to be honest. In 2024 Yeeun Archer Lee, Frances Chen and colleagues at the University of British Columbia ran a two-week trial with 388 university students, assigning them to everyday acts of kindness, to anonymous acts of kindness, or to an active control. Ordinary kindness increased daily social contact, especially with people already close, and reduced day-to-day loneliness among those who started out lonely. Anonymous kindness did not. The authors' reading is that direct contact with the person you are helping may be the active ingredient, at least for loneliness.

A larger study from Sonja Lyubomirsky's lab points the same way. Across 754 employees doing either kind acts or plain social interactions, online or in person, every active group gained in wellbeing and in general connectedness. But when the researchers looked at individual episodes, the ones that generated the most felt connection were with closer people and through non-digital channels. Kindness dropped into a void, with no one on the other side, is the weakest version of the thing.

Kindness helps the giver. Kindness that nobody answers helps less, and does little for loneliness.

Closing the loop

That finding shaped how Kind Messages work in Luvia, and it is why they are not a wall you post onto. You write something you are carrying, or something you want a stranger to hear. It is checked for safety before it enters the pool, then goes to one person, chosen at random, who reads it and writes back. That reply comes to you. Then the exchange closes: no thread, no profile, no handle to look up, no second message. Both sides are anonymous, but neither side is alone with the message. Somebody read it and answered, and you know that they did.

It is a deliberate middle path between the two conditions in the UBC study. It keeps the protection of anonymity, which matters for people who find direct asking hard, and it restores the one thing anonymous kindness was missing, which is a human being acknowledging that it landed. We do not claim it does what a friend does. We do think it is meaningfully different from writing into nothing.

Connection is the mechanism

Two further studies make the same point from different directions. David Cregg and Jennifer Cheavens worked with 122 adults with moderate to severe symptoms of depression or anxiety, comparing two standard cognitive-behavioural exercises with a simple instruction to perform three acts of kindness on two days a week. All three groups improved over ten weeks. The kindness group improved at least as much on symptoms, and was the only group to report feeling more connected to other people, which the authors suggest is part of how it worked.

At the other end of the scale, the KIND Challenge randomised 4,284 adults in the United States, the United Kingdom and Australia to four weeks of at least one kind act a week for a neighbour, or to a waiting list. Loneliness fell by a small but significant amount in the US and the UK, with reductions in social isolation and social anxiety alongside. The kind acts were ordinary: taking in a parcel, a note, a conversation over the fence. The common thread, again, is that someone on the receiving end knew.

A small, safe place to try it

If you want to try this, the research suggests starting close, with people who already know you. A message to a friend you have been meaning to write to will do more than a grand gesture to nobody. When that feels like too much, or when what you need to say is easier said to a stranger, Kind Messages are there for that moment. They are for adults only, every message is moderated before it is delivered, and the things that make anonymous spaces go wrong, such as contact details, threads and identities, are simply not possible. If a reply is worth keeping, you can pin it to the day in your journal and come back to it. It is one small exchange, and then it lets go.

The kinder reading

The temptation with research like this is to turn kindness into another self-improvement task, a thing you do for the effect size. The studies resist that reading. The lift appears precisely when your attention is on the other person rather than on yourself, and it is strongest when they can feel it and say so. Being kind to a stranger helps you, not because you are being clever about your own mood, but because for a moment there were two of you in it.

The short answers

Does being kind actually make you happier?

Modestly, yes. A 2018 meta-analysis of 27 experiments found a small-to-medium improvement in the giver's wellbeing, and a six-week trial found the gain came from kindness to others, not from treating yourself.

Does anonymous kindness help with loneliness?

In a 2024 trial with university students, everyday kindness reduced daily loneliness among lonely participants but anonymous kindness did not. Direct contact with the recipient seems to be the active ingredient for loneliness specifically.

How are Kind Messages different from posting anonymously?

Each message goes to one person, who reads it and replies, and that reply comes back to you. Both sides stay anonymous, with no profiles, threads or contact details, but the exchange is answered rather than dropped into a void.

Are Kind Messages a form of mental health support?

No. They are short supportive notes between adults, moderated for safety, and not therapy, crisis support or a substitute for professional help.
