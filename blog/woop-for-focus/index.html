WOOP for focus: a plan for the moment you reach for your phone.

Most attempts to stop getting distracted fail at the same second. WOOP is a five-minute way to decide, in advance, what happens in that second.

You sit down to work on the thing that matters. Within four minutes your hand has found your phone, and you did not decide to pick it up. Nobody did. That is the ordinary shape of losing focus, and it is why rules like "no phone until lunch" so rarely survive the morning: the rule lives in the part of you that makes decisions, and the reaching happens before that part is consulted.

WOOP is a different kind of answer. Gabriele Oettingen's four-step method takes about five minutes, and rather than asking you to be more disciplined it asks you to find the exact inner moment where your attention leaves, and to decide now what you will do when that moment comes. This post walks the four steps on one concrete focus wish, so you can borrow the shape and swap in your own.

Why focus needs a plan, not a rule

A rule is a general intention: I will not get distracted. Peter Gollwitzer, whose research supplies the second half of WOOP, spent decades showing that general intentions are weak at exactly the moments they are needed, because they require you to notice, deliberate and choose while the distraction is already pulling. An if-then plan moves the choice earlier. You decide at a calm moment, you attach the decision to a cue you can feel arriving, and when the cue arrives the response runs with very little effort. The situation does the remembering. For focus, that is the whole difference, because the lapse is over before willpower has had its say.

You cannot win the argument with your phone in the moment. You can decide it beforehand.

Wish: ninety minutes, one piece of work

The wish should be demanding but within reach, and bounded in time. "Be more focused" is neither. Here is the one we will use: ninety uninterrupted minutes on one piece of work each weekday morning for the next two weeks. Ninety minutes is long enough to matter and short enough to be believable. One piece of work removes the choosing. Weekday mornings give you ten attempts, which is enough to learn from. Two weeks has an end, so the wish is a trial rather than a new identity you have to live up to forever. If ninety feels like a lie, make it sixty. A wish you half believe gives the rest of the method nothing to work with.

Outcome: what the good version feels like

Now picture the best thing about it coming true, and make it an image rather than a summary. Not "I would be more productive" but the specific feeling at eleven o'clock on a Thursday: the document is further along than it was, your chest is not tight, and the rest of the day is yours because the heavy thing already moved. Stay with it a minute, because the next step works by contrast and contrast needs something vivid to push against.

Obstacle: the itch, not the notification

This is where most focus advice goes wrong, and where WOOP is most useful. The tidy answer is "notifications", and you can silence those in thirty seconds. The obstacle WOOP asks for is the one inside you, and for focus it usually comes in one of three flavours. There is the itch to check, a small restlessness that arrives about ninety seconds into anything hard. There is the dread of starting, where the work feels vaguely threatening and the phone is simply the nearest exit. And there is the relief of a small task, where answering one quick email feels like progress and lets you avoid the thing that does not.

Pick the one that is actually yours, and describe it in its first second, the way you experience it from the inside. A slight lean back in the chair. The thought "I will just see if". A flicker of boredom that feels like a reason. The more precisely you can name that first second, the better the next step will fire.

The phone is not the obstacle. It is where the obstacle goes when it leaves you.

Plan: an if-then that fires at the itch

The plan is one sentence, if obstacle then action, and the action has to be something a person watching could see you do. "Then I will stay focused" is not a plan; it is the wish wearing a plan's clothes. For the itch, something like: if I feel the pull to check my phone, then I put both hands on the keyboard and finish the sentence I am in. For the dread of starting: if I notice I am circling the work instead of opening it, then I open the file and write one bad line. For the small-task relief: if I catch myself reaching for email, then I write the task on the pad beside me and return to the document.

Notice what each plan does. It does not fight the itch, and it does not ask you to feel differently. It gives your hands a job for the next ten seconds, which is usually all the itch needs to pass. Write one plan, not four. Then put the phone in another room, not because the plan needs it but because there is no reason to make the cue louder than it has to be.

What the evidence shows, honestly

The honest summary is that this method has good evidence for goal attainment and for study effort, and thinner evidence for focus measured as such. Gollwitzer and Sheeran's 2006 meta-analysis of 94 independent tests found that if-then plans had a medium to large effect on reaching goals across many domains. An earlier line of work by Gollwitzer and Schaal had people solve arithmetic problems while distracting film clips played, and found that the bare intention not to be distracted protected performance less than an if-then plan aimed at the distraction.

The school studies are about effort rather than attention. In 2011, Angela Duckworth and colleagues taught mental contrasting with implementation intentions to high-school students preparing for the PSAT; the group that learned it completed around sixty per cent more practice questions over the summer than the control group. In 2013 the same approach, taught to fifth graders from an urban school, improved report card grades, attendance and conduct compared with a positive-thinking control. None of these measured ninety-minute blocks of concentration. What they show is that the method reliably turns an intention into more of the intended behaviour, which is the thing focus is made of.

After two weeks

Do the woop once, at a quiet moment, and then let it work in the background. You do not repeat it daily. What you do, at the end of the two weeks, is reopen it and ask three plain questions: did the obstacle show up in the form I named, did the plan fire, and did the mornings move. Often the answer reveals that you picked the tidy obstacle after all, and the real one was dread rather than itch. That is not a failed woop. It is the information the next one needs. Luvia keeps past woops together in rituals, so the version you wrote today is still there to reopen, rather than lost in a notebook you have stopped carrying.

WOOP handles the moment attention leaves. If you also want to train the return, a short daily sit does that, and we have written about it in meditation for focus. The two go together well: one decides what happens at the itch, the other makes the itch a little easier to notice in the first place.

The short answers

Can WOOP help me stop getting distracted?

It can help with the specific moment distraction begins, because it asks you to name the inner cue, such as the itch to check your phone, and to decide in advance what you will do when it arrives. The research support is strongest for goal attainment and study effort rather than focus measured directly.

What is a good if-then plan for phone distraction?

Name the first second of the urge, then give your hands a visible action. For example: if I feel the pull to check my phone, then I put both hands on the keyboard and finish the sentence I am in. Write one plan per woop.

Why is the obstacle not simply notifications?

Notifications can be silenced in seconds and the hand still reaches for the phone. WOOP asks for the obstacle inside you, which for focus is usually the itch to check, the dread of starting, or the relief of a small task.

How often should I repeat a focus woop?

Once, at a calm moment, and then let it run. After the period you set, reopen it and check whether the obstacle appeared as you named it and whether the plan fired, then write the next one from what you learned.
